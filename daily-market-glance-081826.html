Rates Rose Again To 6.73% And The 30 Year Treasury Hit A 19 Year High Of 5.31%; The Typical US Home Is Now Worth $371,774 While Fremont's Median Ask Is $1,298,944; Locally, 710 Homes Are Buyable, Down 11

Rates rose for a second straight session. The 30 year fixed added 2 basis points to 6.73% and the 15 year added 1 to 6.30%. The pressure is coming from the bond market, where the 30 year Treasury closed at 5.31%, a level not seen since 2007, and oil jumped 2.65% on the Iran news. Both are in the Economy section, and both push the same way.
Zillow put the typical US home value at $371,774 in July, up 1.1% from a year ago. Set that next to Fremont's median asking price of $1,298,944 and you have the single most useful conversation you can have with a client this week. The national number is not a benchmark for our market. It is a different market entirely, roughly three and a half times smaller.
Zillow also reported that sales surged 7% in July, the strongest annual gain this year, but newly pending listings rose only 0.3%. Strong sales with a flat pipeline is what the end of a run looks like. The homes that are closing now went under contract earlier, and there is less behind them.
• A note on the Gen Z survey: 58% say they want a housing crash so that prices fall far enough to make ownership possible. It is worth answering honestly rather than dismissively, because the frustration is real. A crash does not hand anyone a house. It arrives with job losses and tightened lending, which is precisely when a first time buyer cannot qualify. What actually moves someone from renting to owning is income, savings and help with the down payment. That is a harder answer and a truer one.
Why a REALTOR® cares: two of the most quoted numbers in the country right now, a $371,774 typical home and a hoped for crash, are both misleading for a Fremont buyer. Being the person who can explain why, without condescending to anyone, is the job.

Numbers as of market close on August 17th.
Red across the board. The S&P 500 fell 0.52% to 7,745, the Dow lost 0.51% to 53,459, and the NASDAQ eased 0.32% to 26,644.
Meta was the day's story, down 3.54%. The company is facing a federal trial over claims its apps are designed to harm teenagers. A New Mexico court has already ordered it to pay $942 million, and the case is now moving to California where more penalties could follow. The stakes go past the fines: a loss could force real changes to how Instagram and Facebook are built, and possibly set the template for the rest of social media. The stock is down 13.80% for the year, and the chart below shows a year that has been all round trips and no progress.
Nvidia is backing OpenAI's new Ohio data center with up to $105 billion in credit and computing power. Read that twice. A chip company is extending credit so its customer can afford to buy its chips, which is a bank's job, not a supplier's. It locks in demand, and it also means Nvidia now carries the risk if that demand does not show up.
Google is buying bankrupt airline Spirit's data for $10 million to train its AI. The most valuable thing left in the estate was the list of people who flew.
Japan grew at a 1.1% annualized pace last quarter against a 2% forecast. Exports carried it while domestic spending slowed, and the Nikkei fell 2.54%. The STOXX 600 and the CSI 300 each eased 0.22%.
Meta closed at $568.97 on August 17, down 3.54% on the day and 13.80% for the year, a loss of $91.12 a share. The dashed line marks where the stock ended 2025.

Ten year yield is the Treasury close for August 17th. Brent is the August 17th settlement. Gold quoted at 8:50 AM Eastern and silver at 6:45 AM Eastern on August 18th.
Oil is the number to watch this week. Iran said it will not extend the temporary nuclear deal, and crude climbed on worries about supply through the Strait of Hormuz. Brent settled at $90.87, up 2.65%, and both sides are now talking about escalation. If this holds, it reaches you at the pump first and in the price of everything trucked to a store second.
The newsletter reported Brent at $91.30 and up 0.47%. The settlement was $90.87 and the move was 2.65%, so the level was a little high and the gain was understated by a factor of five. Direction matters more than the decimal here, and the direction is up.
The long end of the bond market is doing something worth noticing. The 30 year Treasury yield closed at 5.31%, its highest since 2007. The newsletter said it crossed 5.28%, which understates it slightly. Either way, the last time borrowing money for thirty years cost this much, the iPhone was new. The 10 year rose to 4.72%, up 4 basis points, and the newsletter again called it unchanged when it was not.
Gold rose to $4,397 and silver eased to $65.12. A rising 30 year yield and a rising gold price at the same time is an unusual pair, and it says the market wants both a higher return for lending long and some insurance against what happens if it does not get paid.
The chart below is a reminder of scale. The US economy is about $32.4 trillion, larger than China's by more than $11 trillion, and larger than the next four economies put together. Worth keeping in view on a day when the news is about a bond yield and a conflict overseas: the ground under all of it is still the largest economy anyone has built.
The US economy is larger than China's by more than $11 trillion, and larger than the next four combined. India sits sixth at $4.15 trillion, close enough to pass the UK soon but not there yet.

Bitcoin is up 1.49% to $64,687, Ether added 0.25% to $1,910, and XRP is flat at a dollar.
Yesterday's wallet leak story got worse, and it is worth a second mention because the lesson is not about crypto. Shipping data leaks have exposed the home addresses of people who bought hardware wallets. Wrench attacks, meaning a thief takes the physical device rather than hacking anything, jumped 75% in 2025, and more than $40 million was stolen that way. The security of the technology was never the weak point. The delivery address was.
Michael Saylor's Strategy has stopped buying Bitcoin for now. It sold $333.7 million of stock last week, but the cash is going toward paying down preferred shares and building a $4.8 billion reserve rather than more coins. When the most committed buyer in the market starts holding cash instead, that is worth noting, whatever you think of the asset.
The throughline for us is boring and useful: the risk usually arrives through the ordinary business record, not the impressive technology. A shipping manifest did more damage here than any exploit.
