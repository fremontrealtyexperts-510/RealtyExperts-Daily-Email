Rates went nowhere, and that is the good news. The daily 30-year fixed held at 6.83% and the 15-year at 6.32%, parked about two basis points under the 52-week high. After last week's climb, flat counts as a win.
Foreclosures are up, and the headline needs context. Filings rose 21% in the first half of 2026 to 227,548 properties, the highest first half since 2019, and foreclosure starts climbed 18%. Economists point to household financial stress rather than anything structural.
Now look at the chart below, because the number only means something next to its history. That 227,548 is about 14% of the 1.65 million filings in the first half of 2010. We are not near a repeat of 2008; we are climbing back toward what a normal housing market looks like, from an artificially frozen floor of 65,082 in 2021 when moratoriums were in place. One more detail worth knowing: the average foreclosure timeline fell to 563 days, the shortest since 2013, so the pipeline is moving faster too.
AI construction is creating a housing niche. Data center crews need somewhere to live fast, and factory built housing is filling the gap. Cavco's orders are booming on exactly that demand, though its shares slid about 5% Friday after missing earnings. The catch is that these crews are large during construction and small once the building opens, so the housing demand is real but temporary.
Why a REALTOR® cares: if a client asks whether rising foreclosures mean a crash is coming, you now have the answer in one chart. Filings are up from a historic low, not down from a peak, and they remain a fraction of crisis levels. Separately, if you serve a distressed seller, a 563 day timeline is still well over a year of runway to work a solution.

Wall Street closed out July on a green note. The NASDAQ added 1.00% to 25,373, the S&P 500 rose 0.70% to 7,489, and the DOW gained 0.53% to 52,485. A quiet, broad advance rather than a headline grabbing one.
Texas got its own exchange. The TXSE opened for business, the first major stock trading venue to launch in the United States in decades, backed by BlackRock, Goldman Sachs, and Citadel Securities. The nickname writes itself: Y'all Street. Keep the scale in perspective though, because the TXSE is valued around $275 million against roughly $44 trillion for the NYSE. It is a statement of intent, not yet a rival.
Traders are piling on against SpaceX. The stock fell 3.41% on Friday, and about 34% of all shares outstanding have now been sold short, which is an unusually aggressive bet for a company that went public less than two months ago. The trigger is a share unlock arriving just after its first earnings report, when up to 911.5 million shares become free to sell. The chart below tells the story: after debuting at $160.95 and spiking to $211.39 four days later, SPCX has given back a third and now trades below its $135 IPO price.
GM is putting its own AI assistant into millions of vehicles this year, going beyond the Google Gemini integration it already added to newer models. Worth noting the company just posted its 16th consecutive earnings beat, so it is spending from a position of strength.
SpaceX has given back about a third of its value since its market debut, closing at $108.37 on July 31, roughly 20% below the $135 price at which the IPO was sold.

Gold, silver, and Brent are live quotes taken the morning of August 3 (change versus the same time a day earlier); the 10-Year is the July 31 Treasury close, and stocks are as of the July 31 close. Crypto is a live quote from this morning. Commodities can move fast intraday, so treat these as a same day snapshot.
The 10-Year keeps climbing. Treasury put the 10-year yield at 4.75% at Friday's close, up 7 basis points from Thursday, and that is the number that actually sets mortgage pricing. Worth flagging that the newsletter reported this one as falling to 4.71%; the Treasury's own daily curve says otherwise, and we go with Treasury.
Metals split. Gold ticked up to $4,051 an ounce while silver slipped to $57.94. Nothing dramatic in either direction after a rough stretch for both.
Oil dropped hard. Brent fell 4.77% to $83.73 a barrel, a genuinely large one day move. The odd part is that gas prices are barely following it down. Exxon and Chevron both warn pump prices will stay high, and the reason is refining, not crude: roughly 5 million barrels of daily refining capacity is offline because of the Russia and Ukraine and the U.S. and Iran conflicts. Cheap oil does not help anyone if there is nowhere to refine it.
Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent argues the economy is stronger than the 1.5% second quarter GDP print suggests, and pins the slowdown on China's moves in rare earths and AI. Read that against the yield rising and you get the tension in one line: officials talking the economy up while the bond market prices in something costlier.
Why a REALTOR® cares: the 10-Year is the single best leading indicator you have for where the 30-year fixed goes next. It rose Friday, which is why rates are sitting a hair under their 52-week high rather than easing. If crude staying cheap eventually cools inflation, that is the path to relief, but refining capacity has to come back first.
