Sunday's market newsletter looked at an old idea: inflation shrinks a debt, because the amount owed stays the same while paychecks grow. Homeowners with a fixed rate mortgage live that math every month. The payment never changes, while incomes and rents rise around it.
Our chart puts local numbers on it. Since 1989, East Bay home values rose about 4.7 times, Alameda County's median household income 3.7 times, and Bay Area consumer prices 2.8 times. Values have cooled lately: this spring's index sits 4.7% below its 2024 average.
Rates are where the national debt reaches your payment. Investors want more to hold long bonds, and the 30-year Treasury touched 5.67% on October 7, its highest since 2002. MND's 30-year mortgage ended the week at 7.37% and the 15-year at 7.04%. On our five city median list price of $1,084,000 with 20% down, principal and interest run about $5,987 a month.
Locally, the five city ledger holds at 679 for a second day, with Fremont at 231 from 233 and Milpitas at 102 from 100. The board wide table eased to 2,662 from 2,666, while coming soon listings rose to 139 from 136.
What this means for you: a buyer who locks a fixed rate today locks the one housing cost inflation cannot raise, with a refinance available if rates fall. Sellers face a ledger where 171 homes have sat 60 days or longer, so pricing to the comps still wins.

Markets are closed for the weekend. Gold and silver are Friday, October 16 settlements (COMEX December); Brent is Friday's ICE December settlement; the 10-Year is Friday's Treasury close.
Sunday's Market Briefs asked whether inflation could help shrink the $40 trillion federal debt. The backdrop checks out: interest on the debt is running about $1.28 trillion a year, and gross debt was 122.6% of the economy in early 2026, just above the 1946 peak of 119%. The newsletter said about 125%; the Fed's data says a little less.
The historical version was the 1940s, when long rates were held near 2.5% while inflation ran far higher, a quiet loss for lenders that economists call financial repression. Today is the opposite. In September the 10-Year paid about 1.3 points more than inflation, positive every month since June 2023, and the Fed's next decision is October 28. For once, the saver is not the one footing the bill; the flip side is mortgage rates above 7%.
In the 1940s, the 1970s and 2022, the 10-Year paid less than inflation. Since June 2023 it has paid more, about 1.3 points in September.
