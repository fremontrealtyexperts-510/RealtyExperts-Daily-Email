Mortgage Rates Tick Back Up To 6.66% As Fed-Hike Bets Firm; The Senate Moves To Curb Wall Street Landlords; The East Bay Board Eases To 2,929 Active On 220 New And 212 Closed
The 30-year fixed ticked back up to 6.66% and the 15-year to 6.20%, as firming bets on Federal Reserve rate hikes nudged borrowing costs higher. That is still roughly double the rate most homeowners are sitting on, which keeps the lock-in effect firmly in place.
Two moves out of Washington matter for housing. The Senate advanced a bill to slow big Wall Street firms from buying up homes, capping institutional investors at 350 single-family homes and easing some building rules. It lands as the country sits more than 4.7 million homes short. Separately, a new Tax Justice Network report named the U.S. the world's top destination for laundering money through real estate, because buyers can hold property through shell companies without revealing who owns them (Canada ranked second, the U.K. eighth).
Locally, the East Bay board eased to 2,929 active (down 27 from yesterday), with 220 new listings against 212 closings. Oakland again led the board with 53 new on 771 active; Fremont added 19 new on 360 active.

Stocks closed mixed: the S&P 500 slipped 0.37% to 7,472 and the NASDAQ fell 1.32% to 26,166, while the DOW edged up 0.29% to 51,712 (figures as of the June 22nd close).
The headline name was SpaceX (SPCX -16.43%), which fell for a third straight session and shed roughly $600 billion in market value. Wild stat: CEO Elon Musk could lose $1 trillion and still rank as the 20th richest person on Earth.
The bigger story is where the money is flowing, which is artificial intelligence. Microsoft (MSFT -3.18%) is building a Texas data center that will run on natural gas instead of the grid, with Chevron (CVX +0.82%) fueling it for 20 years, enough power for more than 530,000 homes. AI data centers need constant power the traditional grid cannot keep up with, which is pushing builders toward gas and other fuels. U.S. data center construction spending now sits at a record high near $42 billion a year, roughly four times where it was before ChatGPT launched (see the chart below).
Dealmakers stayed busy too. AbbVie (ABBV +6.25%) agreed to buy biotech Apogee (APGE +46.66%) for $10.9 billion, and CRH picked up infrastructure firm Arcosa (ACA +6.67%) for $8.5 billion, as companies join forces ahead of possible rate cuts and to scale for the AI era.
Where the AI money is going. U.S. data center construction spending crept along near $10 billion a year for a decade, then exploded after ChatGPT launched in late 2022 to a record near $42 billion. That is the buildout behind deals like Microsoft's new gas-powered Texas data center.

10-Year and Brent as of June 22nd (Market Briefs); gold and silver are live spot, June 23rd (Trading Economics and Fortune).
Oil came back to earth. Brent crude eased to $77.77 (-0.18%) and has tumbled off its spring highs near $114 a barrel (see the chart below). The reason: with the U.S. blockade on Iran lifted, Iran is now dumping crude at roughly $2.50 to $5 a barrel below Brent and supply is flooding back. Some buyers are holding off, so about 121 million barrels are still sitting on tankers. More supply plus slow buyers points to softer prices ahead, and jet fuel has already fallen more than 40% from its April high (even if airfares have not followed yet).
The metals went the other way, and hard. Gold dropped 1.49% to about $4,129 an ounce and silver fell roughly 6.5% to $62.03, as firm expectations for Federal Reserve rate hikes pulled money out of both. Longer term, central banks are still on their biggest gold-buying spree in modern history, with gold now a larger share of their reserves than U.S. Treasuries for the first time since 1996.
The 10-year Treasury yield held near 4.48%. The takeaway for housing: cheaper oil cools inflation at the pump, but a Fed that is leaning toward higher for longer is what keeps mortgage rates sticky.
Brent crude has come back to earth. After spiking near $114 a barrel this spring, oil has slid to $77.77 as the lifted U.S. blockade lets Iranian supply flood back at $2.50 to $5 below Brent. Cheaper oil means cheaper gas and jet fuel, and a little less pressure on inflation.
