Rates Push To 6.75% Even As 10-Year Reverses; Pending Home Sales Hit Third Straight Monthly Gain (74.8 In April); Rent Has Climbed 65% Over Last Decade
Three threads to carry into client conversations today.
First, rates ticked up again to 6.75% on the 30-year (up 7 bps from Tuesday's 6.68%), and 6.25% on the 15-year (up 13 bps). The notable detail: this happened even though the 10-year Treasury reversed 19 bps lower overnight, from 4.85% back to 4.66%. The MBS-Treasury spread is widening, which means lenders are pricing in more rate-volatility risk on every quote. For clients, the practical read is that waiting for the 10-year to fall further does not automatically translate into a lower mortgage rate. The spread is now where the cost is moving.
Second, the Pending Home Sales Hot Streak continued (chart above). The NAR Pending Home Sales Index rose to 74.8 in April, the third straight monthly gain (Feb 72.6, Mar 73.8, Apr 74.8). Signings are now 3.2% higher than last spring. The regional pattern is informative: the Northeast led with a 6.6% monthly jump, while the South actually fell. Translation: the buyers coming off the fence are concentrated in the higher-rate-sensitive parts of the country where the spring inventory build was most pronounced. For Bay Area listings, this is the demand-side confirmation that pairs with the supply-side recovery we have been tracking: pending contracts are moving even with the 30-year at 6.75%. The buy decision is not waiting for cuts.
Third, the Saving Vs. Rent picture (chart above) is the one to put in front of any tenant client who is leaning toward renewing rather than buying. Average U.S. monthly rent has gone from $1,029 in 2016 to $1,698 in 2026, a 65% increase over the decade with no plateau in sight. The Bank of America survey out today shows 17% of Gen Z is spending more than half of every paycheck on housing. Gen Z is becoming financially independent (only 34% still get help from parents, down from 46%), but the rent climb is structurally eating that gain. The mortgage-vs-rent math has shifted: a 6.75% mortgage on a fixed payment beats year-over-year rent escalation if a tenant plans to stay anywhere for more than 4-5 years. That is the conversation today.

For listing agents: the supply pullback today is the seller side of the rate story. When 30-year hit 6.75% and the 15-year jumped 13 bps in a session, sellers in Danville, Livermore, Pleasanton, and San Ramon froze. That is the wrong reaction. Closings are up (260 from 245). Pending home sales are on a three-month national gain streak. The demand is there. The sellers who hold today are giving the active buyers a thinner board to choose from, which raises competitive pressure on every remaining listing. The seller advice today is the same as Monday: list now, price to today, market to closers.
For buyer agents: 210 new listings is the lowest mid-week reading of the week so far. All three indices closed lower Tuesday as the macro repricing kept pressure on equities. S&P 7,353 (-0.67%), DOW 49,363 (-0.65%), NASDAQ 25,870 (-0.84%). Two retail-side reads worth carrying into client conversations. First, Home Depot (HD +0.88%) beat earnings but reported its fourth straight quarter of falling foot traffic. The company is leaning further into pro-contractor sales, which now make up half of total revenue. Translation: even housing-adjacent retail is feeling the consumer slowdown, and HD is pivoting to commercial demand to compensate. Second, CAVA (CAVA -2.22%) grew traffic and raised its 2026 outlook in a quarter when the broader restaurant industry lost customers for eight consecutive months. CAVA hiked prices to manage rising production costs and still grew its customer base, the cleanest indicator yet that consumers are willing to pay premium for differentiated value while abandoning the middle of the market.

The macro tape flipped overnight. The 10-year is back to 4.66%, down 19 basis points from Monday's 4.85% spike. WTI Crude cratered another 5.48% intraday to $98.66, the first sub-$100 print in over a week and a 22% drawdown from the early-May high above $112. Brent at $110.65 (-0.57%). Gold at $4,483 (-0.61%). The oil-down-bonds-down combination is the classic disinflation trade reasserting itself after Monday's hot-data reaction.
The key labor-market story today is AT&T (T +2.25%). The phone giant is spending up to $80,000 per new blue-collar technician and plans to hire 3,000 techs this year on top of the 10,000 added since 2023. Meanwhile, Stanford research found new grads in AI-exposed roles saw 16% slower job growth than peers over the last few years. The U.S. is now short 350,000 construction workers in 2026. That is the structural labor-market story worth carrying: the AI-exposed cohort is losing ground while the trades-exposed cohort is being courted with five-figure training bonuses. For real estate, the construction-labor shortage is the supply-side reason that builder confidence sitting at 37 still has room to run.
For rate-watchers, the divergence to watch is mortgage rates ticking up to 6.75% on the 30-year even as the 10-year backed off 19 bps. That is MBS spreads widening, which means the rate-volatility itself is now the cost: lenders are pricing in more uncertainty into every quote regardless of where the 10-year sits today.

The structural story to watch this week: the SEC is drafting a rule that would let crypto platforms trade tokenized versions of U.S. stocks like Apple (AAPL +0.38%) and Microsoft (MSFT -1.44%). Tokens could be issued by third parties without the company's permission, and Coinbase (COIN +2.12%) and other crypto venues could host U.S. equity trading without registering as full broker-dealers. If this rule lands, the structural distinction between equity and crypto exchanges starts to collapse. For clients asking about the longer-arc crypto thesis, this is a meaningful regulatory data point regardless of the day-to-day price tape.
On the enforcement side, a crypto trader just received nine years in federal prison for a $10M Ponzi scheme that promised high returns on Bitcoin trades and then used new investor money to pay older investors. The U.S. sentencing posture on retail crypto fraud is clearly hardening.
