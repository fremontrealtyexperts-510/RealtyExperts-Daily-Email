Today's theme is the household budget. New federal data show housing and food took 46.9% of the average American household's spending in 2025, the most in 15 years (chart). Nearly half the budget now goes to the roof and the refrigerator. Housing did nearly all of that work: it rose 5.8% to $27,790 a year, and shelter alone rose 8.0%, while total spending rose 4.3%. Market Briefs pinned it on groceries, but its 4.6% is all food, and housing added $1,524 a year to food's $464.
It runs higher here. The latest metro data, for 2023 and 2024, put the San Francisco metro, which includes Alameda County, at $42,517 a year on housing, 36.2% of spending, and 48.6% with food (chart). A Fremont buyer at today's $1,346,900 median with 20% down would pay about $7,365 a month in principal and interest at 7.27%, or $88,380 a year. That is about double the metro average before taxes and insurance.
Rates held, for now. Mortgage News Daily's 30-year average is unchanged at 7.27% and the 15-year at 6.92%. Oil's plunge pulled the 10-Year to about 4.88% this morning from 4.94%, and if that lasts, mortgage rates should follow. It is one morning, and nothing is signed (see Economy).
One headline to read twice: Census said residential construction spending fell 5.5% in September. All of that was home improvements, which had jumped in August. Spending on new single family homes was flat at a $403 billion annual pace.
The board wide table is 2,482, from 2,554 on Friday, with 144 coming soon and 138 brand new. The five city ledger is 650, from 665: Fremont (220), Hayward (183), Milpitas (99) and Newark (84) eased, while Union City (64) rose. Part of that looks like the calendar. The count has dipped at each of the last six month turns, and 63 of the 119 listings that left the board table since Friday were active, not pending, which usually means expired or withdrawn rather than sold.
What this means for you: budget the whole payment, not the list price. Buyers should run principal, interest, taxes, insurance and any HOA against take home pay before they start touring; 274 of the 583 homes on the market in our five cities are priced under a million. Sellers, the buyer across the table is doing that same math, and 179 homes have been sitting 60 days or longer, so price to the payment.

Stocks are higher as oil falls. As of 10:20 AM PT the Dow is up 1.06%, the S&P 500 up 0.42% and the Nasdaq up 0.37%. October was rough on tech: the Nasdaq lost 5.3%, its worst month since March 2025, and the S&P 500 lost 0.9%, while the Dow gained 1.7% as investors rotated toward defensive names. This week brings the midterms and the jobs report.
Big Oil just had a very good quarter. Exxon earned $15.4 billion, up from $7.5 billion a year ago, and Chevron earned $9.9 billion, up from $3.5 billion. Refining did the heavy lifting: Exxon's Energy Products unit made $7.2 billion, from $1.8 billion, and Chevron's downstream made $4.9 billion, from $1.1 billion. Today's oil drop is the other side of that trade. Exxon is down 3.5% and Chevron 3.1% this morning.

Levels are Friday, October 30: the 10-Year is Treasury's close, gold and silver are COMEX December settlements, and Brent is the ICE January settlement. January became the front month when December expired Friday at $117.64, so the lower card is a contract roll, not a drop. Live at 10:20 AM PT Monday: 10-Year 4.88%, gold $3,975.60 (+1.97%), silver $51.01 (+3.52%), Brent $103.37 (-8.87%).
Oil is the story. Brent is down about 9% this morning after Iran's president said Tehran will try to resolve the conflict through dialogue and its foreign minister said negotiators in Qatar are working on a proposal that could reopen the Strait of Hormuz in as little as two days if Iran's conditions are met. Nothing is signed, and a similar offer in September went nowhere. Cheaper oil would ease the inflation that has kept mortgage rates above 7%, which is why the 10-Year fell to about 4.88%. Factories stayed busy: the ISM manufacturing index rose to 55.5 in October from 54.5, with its prices index at 75.2 from 77.9.
I bonds got a raise (chart). Treasury's new rate is 5.51%, up from 4.26%, the highest since the window that ended in April 2023, for bonds bought through April 30. The 1.40% fixed part, which stays for the life of the bond, is the highest since November 2006. The limits matter: $10,000 a year per person, no cashing in for 12 months, and you lose the last three months of interest if you cash in before five years. For a buyer saving a down payment, that is a slow lane, not a checking account.
One correction: Market Briefs stamped gold at $3,910, Brent at $109.69 and the 10-Year at 4.92% as Friday's 4 PM ET levels. The gold and Brent figures were Monday's overnight prices, measured against Friday's settlements. Treasury's Friday close was 4.94%.
New I bonds pay 5.51% for bonds issued through April 30, 2027, up from 4.26%. The 1.40% fixed part is the highest since November 2006.
