Mortgage Rates Jump To 6.66% After A Hot Jobs Report; East Bay Board Dips To 2,912 Active As A Busy Monday Brings 258 New And 217 Closed
The 30-year fixed jumped to 6.66% and the 15-year to 6.13%, the sharpest move up in a while — and the reason is Friday's hot jobs report. A net 172,000 new jobs in May, with firm wage growth, pushed the 10-year Treasury to 4.53% and knocked down the odds of near-term Fed rate cuts (see Economy). Mortgage rates take their cue from that 10-year, so they followed it higher. Wednesday's CPI print is the next swing factor; for now, the buyer who was waiting for rates to break lower got the opposite.
Locally, the East Bay board dipped to 2,912 active, down 23 from Friday's 2,935 — but the activity underneath was anything but quiet: 258 new listings hit and 217 sales closed over the long weekend. The new-listing surge is partly a Monday effect — a weekend's worth of fresh inventory posting at once — while the jump in closings shows buyers kept transacting. More homes came on than cleared, yet the board still edged down, a sign plenty of listings also moved into contract.
A national note for clients: the Trump administration is again pushing to take Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac public — the two government-backed giants stand behind roughly 70% of all U.S. mortgages — though the effort may stall now that FHFA Director Bill Pulte has been tapped as acting Director of National Intelligence and simply won't have the bandwidth. Any privatization would be the biggest structural change to mortgage finance in years, so it's worth watching even if nothing happens overnight.

Friday was brutal for stocks. The S&P 500 fell 2.64% and the NASDAQ tumbled 4.18% — their worst single day since 2025 — while the DOW held up comparatively well at -1.35%. The damage was concentrated in the AI trade: chip stocks led the selling as the names that had been setting record highs went into sharp reverse.
The poster child was Meta (META -5.51%), which dropped more than 5% after saying it would sell more stock to fund its AI build-out. Issuing new shares to raise cash dilutes existing shareholders — each share becomes a slightly smaller slice of the company — and with AI spending already under scrutiny, investors used it as a reason to sell. Elsewhere, several states signaled they may sue to block Paramount's (PSKY -4.31%) takeover of Warner Bros. (WBD -2.81%), a reminder that even a federally approved merger can stall at the state level.

The jobs market ran hot, and that complicates the rate picture. The first employment report under new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh showed a net 172,000 jobs added in May, well above expectations, with wage growth firm enough to keep inflation pressure alive. Strong hiring is good news for households, but it makes near-term rate cuts less likely — markets had been counting on cooling to force the Fed's hand.
The inflation test comes Wednesday, when the May CPI lands. Headline inflation is expected to push above 4%, even as core inflation (which strips out food and energy) is forecast to cool month-over-month — a split that may not last, since today's higher energy and input costs tend to seep into goods and services a few months later. The 10-year Treasury climbed to 4.53% on the jobs strength, while gold fell hard to $4,354 (-3.35%) and Brent crude eased to $93.09.

Crypto bucked the stock-market gloom. Bitcoin firmed 2.3% to $62,240, with Ether ($1,634, +4.3%) and XRP ($1.14, +4.6%) posting bigger gains — a rare session where digital assets rose while equities sank. Big banks are leaning in, too: JPMorgan, Citigroup, and Bank of America are jointly building a 'tokenized deposit' network that would let ordinary bank deposits settle in seconds rather than the hours or days a wire can take.
The caution flag is in the ETF data. Spot Bitcoin ETFs are bleeding cash — BlackRock's iShares Bitcoin Trust (IBIT) alone shed more than $3.1 billion recently — a reminder that wrapping Bitcoin in an ETF also hands investors an easy sell button. For Bay Area buyers and sellers the tie-in is indirect, but crypto swings still color the risk appetite of a slice of the market.
