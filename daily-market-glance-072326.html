The daily 30-year fixed jumped to 6.85%, its highest since June of last year, and the 15-year rose to 6.29%. Rates have been grinding higher alongside oil and inflation worries.
Here is the twist: buyers are not all backing down. With rates up, plenty of sellers are trimming asking prices and more homes are hitting the market, which hands buyers real room to negotiate. The affordability math is still brutal, though. The ICE Mortgage Technology index suggests it would take about a 16% drop in home prices, a 19% jump in incomes, or mortgage rates back near 5% to get affordability back to normal, and none of those is coming soon. So the opportunity right now is less about cheaper prices and more about less competition and more willing sellers.

The headliners reported after the bell. Google beat on profit but told investors it needs to spend even more on AI to hit its goals. Gold and silver are live spot quotes taken the morning of July 23 (change versus the same time a day earlier); the 10-Year, Brent, stocks, and crypto are as of the July 22 close. Metals can move fast intraday, so treat these as a morning snapshot.
Retiring is getting more expensive. Fidelity now estimates a 65-year-old leaving the workforce in 2026 will spend an average of about $185,500 on healthcare in retirement, and this year's estimate jumped roughly 7%, the biggest one-year increase in the series (chart below). Long-term care is not even in that number.
Oil kept climbing. Brent crude rose 1.85% to $95.85 as Middle East supply worries lingered. The 10-Year Treasury held at 4.65%.
The metals cooled off. Gold slipped back below $4,100, down about 2.1% to $4,046, and silver fell roughly 3.2% to $57.90 after a strong run, as traders booked profits and weighed the odds of Fed cuts.
Why a REALTOR® cares about retiree healthcare: it is one more fixed cost competing for the same dollars as a mortgage. For move-up sellers and downsizing retirees especially, a bigger medical reserve can change how much house makes sense.
Fidelity's annual retiree health care cost estimate jumped about 7% this year, the biggest one-year increase in the series. A 65-year-old retiring in 2026 now faces an average of roughly $185,500 in lifetime medical costs, and that excludes long-term care.
