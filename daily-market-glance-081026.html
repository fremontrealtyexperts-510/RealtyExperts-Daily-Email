Rates came down. The 30-year fixed fell 3 basis points to 6.74% and the 15-year dropped 5 to 6.25%, both on Friday's weak jobs report. Today's newsletter printed the same two numbers, which matches Mortgage News Daily exactly.
The negotiating position has shifted, and this is the number to bring to clients. Redfin says homes sold below asking in 38 of the 50 biggest U.S. metros, and only 25% of homes now sell above asking. For anyone who spent the last several years telling buyers to expect competition, that advice is out of date in most of the country. The cause is supply: places like Florida and Texas built heavily, and buyers there now have real choice.
Be careful applying that nationally, though, because our market is not those markets. The Bay Area did not overbuild. What we have is a slow thaw, not a glut, and you can see it in the local numbers below: inventory drifted DOWN since Friday while the coming soon pipeline grew. Those two things together describe a market with limited supply and sellers waiting for a better moment, which is close to the opposite of Tampa or Austin.
Homebuilders are consolidating. Dream Finders is buying Beazer for $916 million in an all-cash deal, and Berkshire Hathaway bought Taylor Morrison in late July for $6.8 billion. Two large acquisitions inside a month is a pattern, not a coincidence. When building slows, the cheapest way to add homes and land is to buy a company that already has both, rather than start new projects at these rates.
Why a REALTOR® cares: Friday delivered the thing buyers have been waiting on, and it came in the least comfortable way possible. Rates fell because hiring went negative. If you have a client on the fence, the honest framing is that their borrowing cost improved because the labor market weakened, and those two facts do not cancel out. A slightly cheaper payment is worth less to someone who is newly worried about their job. Ask about the job first.

Friday closed green across the board. The NASDAQ led, up 1.30% to 26,691, the S&P 500 added 0.62% to 7,758, and the Dow rose 0.28% to 54,037. A weak jobs report usually knocks stocks down. This time it did the opposite, because a soft labor market makes a Fed rate cut more likely, and cheaper money is worth more to the market than strong hiring is.
Airbnb was the standout, up 17.43% after beating expectations and crediting AI for the gains. Its CEO said AI is letting the company ship product updates faster without adding staff, and that it plans to spend a great deal more on it. That is the version of the AI story investors are paying up for right now: not a new product, just the same work done with fewer people.
Berkshire Hathaway spent $4.5 billion buying back its own stock last quarter, against roughly $235 million in the first quarter. Greg Abel took over from Warren Buffett earlier this year and inherited close to $400 billion in cash. A buyback of that size is the new chief executive saying he would rather own more of Berkshire than hold the cash, which is the first real read anyone has gotten on how he intends to operate.
The White House put a 15% tariff on imported polysilicon, the raw material in solar panels and computer chips. Domestic producers get a price umbrella, and solar names moved on it: Sunrun rose 8.74%, Enphase 5.55%, and First Solar 2.42%. Worth remembering that last week the same sector was falling because Treasury had not finalized the residential tax-credit rules. The policy is pushing this industry around in both directions.
One small sign of the times. Wendy's rose 4.60% even after cutting its dividend, and a study found the average burger, fries, and drink combo now runs more than $15 in most places.

Gold and silver are morning Eastern quotes on August 10 measured against the same hour a day earlier, so they span a thin weekend market. Brent is the August 7 ICE front-month settlement. Stocks are as of the August 7 close and crypto is a live quote from this morning.
The July jobs report came out Friday and it was the first negative month in years. Employers cut 23,000 jobs. Forecasters had expected a gain of about 83,000, so the miss was more than 100,000 either way you count it.
Read one layer down, because the headline is misleading in an important way. Government payrolls fell 53,000 while private employers actually added 30,000. So the negative number is a government story, not a collapse of the private economy. Leisure and hospitality shed about 40,000, which is the part worth watching, since that sector hires quickly when consumers feel good and stops just as fast when they do not.
The unemployment rate fell to 4.1%, and this is the second thing the headline gets wrong. It did not fall because people found work. It fell because people stopped looking, and someone who is not looking is not counted as unemployed. A jobless rate improving for that reason is a warning, not good news.
Wages tell the same story. Average hourly earnings are up 3.2% over the past year, the smallest increase since May 2021. When employers stop competing for workers, they stop raising pay.
The newsletter reported the 10-Year at 4.64%, down 0.02. Treasury's own daily curve says it closed Friday at 4.65%, down 0.04 on the day, and we go with Treasury. Either way the direction is what matters: money moved into bonds on the jobs news, which pulls yields down.
Gold is $4,334 an ounce this morning, off 0.22% from the same hour yesterday, and silver is $63.92, up 0.29%. Both readings span the weekend, when metals trade thin. The newsletter printed gold at $4,401 as of Friday afternoon, which is a different moment rather than a different opinion: gold spiked on the jobs report and has given some of it back since. Silver was left out of the report for the fourth day running. Brent settled at $83.55, up 1.29%, still carrying the Strait of Hormuz risk premium from late last week.
Separately, the White House is again trying to remove Federal Reserve governor Lisa Cook, accusing her of misstatements on mortgage paperwork. The Supreme Court shut down the earlier attempt. It matters here only for what it signals: pressure on the Fed to cut faster.
Why a REALTOR® cares: last Thursday I wrote that soft jobs data kept failing to move mortgage rates, and that you should not promise clients relief on a schedule. Friday is what it looks like when the mechanism finally works. Payrolls went negative, the 10-Year fell, and mortgage rates followed it down. That is the sequence. It just does not run on a calendar.

A soft weekend. Bitcoin is near $64,185, Ether is around $1,876, and XRP is just over $1.02, all three down between 1.5% and 2.6% over the past day. Note that these are live Monday morning quotes, so they are lower than the Friday afternoon figures the newsletter printed.
The bigger picture is that the major coins are sitting more than 40% below their October record highs, and the industry is hunting for a new story. The one it has settled on is AI. The pitch is that software agents, not people, will be the next wave of users, paying and trading on their own, and exchanges including Coinbase and Kraken are building tools aimed at machines rather than humans. Coinbase rose 5.63% on Friday. Whether or not the thesis holds, it tells you the sector no longer expects retail enthusiasm to carry it.
Regulators had a win. The U.S. cut off an Iran-linked exchange that had moved more than $6.3 billion for sanctioned parties. The detail worth noting is how it was caught: public blockchains record every transaction permanently, so the same transparency crypto is often criticized for is exactly what made the money traceable.
