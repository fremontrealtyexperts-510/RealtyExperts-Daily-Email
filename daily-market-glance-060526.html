Mortgage Rates Ease To 6.58% Even As Consumer Sentiment Hits A Record Low; East Bay Inventory Builds To 2,935 Active On A Brisk 229-New Friday
The 30-year fixed eased to 6.58% and the 15-year to 6.11%, slipping a few basis points after ticking up earlier in the week. The move lower is modest and a little surprising given the backdrop: consumer sentiment just hit a record low and the prices businesses pay for services jumped to a three-year high (see Economy). But the 10-year Treasury held steady at 4.47%, and mortgage rates followed it sideways-to-lower rather than chasing the inflation headlines. The takeaway for buyers is unchanged — rates are grinding in a narrow band, not breaking decisively in either direction.
Locally, the East Bay board climbed to 2,935 active, up 34 from Thursday's 2,901, on a brisk 229 new listings and 200 closed sales. The inventory build continued and even accelerated a touch: new supply rose (229 vs 214) while closings cooled (200 vs 204), so noticeably more homes are coming on than are clearing.
A national note worth passing along: the world is betting on America's housing shortage. Japan's largest homebuilders are buying their way in — Sekisui House paid $4.9 billion for MDC Holdings (parent of Richmond American Homes), Sumitomo Forestry closed a $4.5 billion deal for Tri Pointe Homes, and Daiwa House already owns Stanley Martin Homes. Add Berkshire Hathaway's long-standing housing bets, and the message is that deep-pocketed money sees years of undersupply ahead — a useful data point when a buyer worries they're overpaying for a market that's 'due' to fall.

The major indexes split on Thursday. The DOW jumped 1.73% to 51,562 and the S&P 500 added 0.41%, while the NASDAQ slipped a hair (-0.09%) — a broadly green day led by old-economy names.
Two earnings reactions stood out for the same counterintuitive reason: a strong report isn't always enough. Five Below (FIVE -13.78%) beat estimates and raised its outlook, yet the stock fell nearly 14% — after a big run-up, investors had already priced in good news and wanted more. CrowdStrike (CRWD -3.81%) told a similar story: it beat on recurring revenue, lifted guidance, and announced a stock split, but shares still dipped. A stock split simply divides each share into more, lower-priced shares — like trading a $20 bill for two $10s. It doesn't change the company's value, just makes the per-share price more accessible, so on its own it's no reason for the stock to move.

The growth-versus-inflation tug-of-war got sharper. The U.S. services sector — the biggest slice of the economy — sped up in May, but the prices businesses paid jumped to their highest level since August 2022, and hiring fell for a third straight month. Growth is still alive, in other words, but inflation is steadily eating into it.
The mood among households is grim. The University of Michigan's consumer-sentiment index sank to 44.8, the lowest reading on record, with more than half of Americans saying high prices are eroding their finances — climbing gas prices are a big part of why. Overseas, Japan is wrestling with a weak yen: it has spent roughly $74 billion defending the currency, which still sits near 160 to the dollar, and the Bank of Japan may now resort to its first meaningful rate hikes toward levels unseen since 1995. The 10-year Treasury held at 4.47% and gold eased slightly to $4,496.

Crypto drifted lower across the board. Bitcoin slipped 0.9% to $63,210, with Ether ($1,752, -1.0%) and XRP ($1.15, -1.1%) sliding in step. Ether has now fallen more than 20% since one prominent strategist declared a 'crypto spring' in early May — a reminder of how quickly sentiment can turn. One crypto-heavy company, Bitmine Immersion (BMNR), is sitting on an $8.9 billion paper loss on its Ether holdings.
On the policy front, JPMorgan warns that Congress is running low on time to pass the CLARITY Act, a bill meant to set clearer rules for U.S. crypto markets, with midterm-election season threatening to push it further out. For Bay Area buyers and sellers the read-through is indirect, but crypto swings still color the risk appetite of a slice of the market, so it's worth keeping on the radar.
