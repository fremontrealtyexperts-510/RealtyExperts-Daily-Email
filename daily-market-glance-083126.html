The 30 year fixed is 6.81% and the 15 year is 6.35%, both unchanged, per Mortgage News Daily, whose board posts its Monday reading late this afternoon. Worth noting where those sit: the 15 year is exactly at the top of its 52 week range, and the 30 year is four basis points off the top of its own.
The important shift this morning is in what the Fed is being expected to do. Coming out of Jackson Hole, the newsletter reports markets now pricing roughly a 50% chance of a rate hike in September, up from about 35% a week ago, with Chair Warsh saying inflation is not falling fast enough toward the 2% target. Treasury's ten year backs that up: it jumped six basis points on Friday to 4.73%.
Read that plainly, because it changes the advice. For most of this year the question a client asked was how long to wait for a cut. The question now is whether the next move is up. Anyone sitting on the fence for a better rate should understand that the market has stopped assuming the fence gets more comfortable.
ONE NEWSLETTER CLAIM TO SET STRAIGHT BEFORE ANYONE REPEATS IT
This morning's issue says outbound home shopping "exceeded 60% nationally" in the second quarter and that nearly all prospective Silicon Valley buyers are looking elsewhere. Those numbers do not match what Redfin actually publishes. Its migration series has 19.1% of house hunters looking to move to a different metro, and its most recent published quarter is the first quarter of 2026, not the second.
There is a real story underneath it, just a smaller one. San Jose did post the fourth largest net outflow of prospective buyers in the country, behind New York, Seattle and Los Angeles, with Sacramento the most common destination. Redfin also notes Bay Area outbound migration has slowed considerably from its 2021 and 2022 peak as return to office and the artificial intelligence hiring wave pulled people back. That is close to the opposite of "nearly all buyers are leaving."

Ten year yield and Brent are Friday's August 28 settlements. Gold and silver are Fortune spot prints at 9:05 AM Eastern today, and both are measured against their August 28 prints rather than against the weekend. Neither metal is falling this morning: see the commentary.
Friday's warning came true, so let us be precise about what those metals cards mean.
On Friday we published gold at $4,608 and silver at $70.62, both from the 6:30 AM print, and flagged in this section that both had reversed hard once the US session opened. They had. Gold now prints $4,438 and silver $67.35, down 3.69% and 4.63% from Friday morning. Almost all of that happened Friday afternoon, not today. As this is written gold futures are at $4,482 against Friday's $4,478 settlement and silver futures are at $67.09 against $67.00, so both metals are flat this morning. If you quoted Friday's gold to a client over the weekend, that is the correction.
The ten year is the number that actually moved. Treasury's daily curve has it at 4.73% for Friday, up six basis points from Thursday's 4.67%. This morning's newsletter printed 4.70% and called it down a basis point, which is wrong in both level and direction, and a six basis point jump on a Friday is not a rounding matter. It is the second time in three sessions the newsletter has missed this one.
Brent settled at $89.31, down 0.43%. The newsletter reported $90.43 and a 2.61% gain. Direction wrong again.
The grain chart below is the one to spend a minute on, because both the newsletter and the graphic circulating with it have the numbers wrong in an instructive way. Wheat did not settle at $7.84 a bushel on Friday. It settled at $7.67, up 51.3% on the year. The $7.84 figure is this morning's intraday high, printed while the pit was still open and then labeled as a Friday settlement. Corn is the bigger stretch: it settled Friday at $5.12, up 16.3%, and it was not at a multi year high at all, having closed higher two sessions earlier. Corn only clears its July 2023 mark on this morning's live tick, which has not settled and may not hold.
What is true, and it is quite enough: wheat is at its highest settlement since February 2023, and essentially the whole move is a fortnight old. Bread and cereal prices follow with a lag, and this is the sort of thing that keeps a core inflation reading sticky just as the Fed has started talking about hikes rather than cuts.
Wheat is up 51.3% on the year and corn 16.3%, measured at Friday's settlement. Nearly all of it happened in the last two weeks.

A quiet morning. Bitcoin has clawed back above $78,000 after Friday's selling, and the majors are within a quarter of a percent of flat.
The story this morning is the companies that hold Bitcoin rather than the coin, and it needs unpacking. The headline going around says Bitcoin treasury stocks have lost $83 billion in market value over the past year, illustrated with the combined value of the 50 largest listed holders falling from $150 billion to $67 billion. The newsletter carried the figure that the graphic leaves out: Strategy alone accounts for $79 billion of that $83 billion. This is not fifty companies sagging together. It is one company losing almost all of it while the other forty nine lose about $4 billion between them.
The chart below shows the mechanism, and every line in it can be checked. Since the end of July last year Strategy is down 68% while Bitcoin is down 32%. The stock fell roughly twice as far as the asset it exists to hold. That gap is the whole thesis breaking: the shares used to trade at a premium to the coins on the balance sheet, and the premium is what went away.
One correction. The newsletter says Bitcoin is "still down 28% year to date." It closed 2025 at $87,509 and sits near $78,400, which is down about 10% on the year, not 28%.
Strategy is down 68% since July 2025 while Bitcoin is down 32%. The proxy fell about twice as hard as the asset.
