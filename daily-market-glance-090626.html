California's housing market lost ground on almost every measure C.A.R. tracked last week, and yet every city on this side of the bay still went under contract faster than the state and still sold closer to its asking price. Here is that gap, in C.A.R.'s own numbers.
CALIFORNIA COOLED AGAIN LAST WEEK
C.A.R.'s weekly scorecard for the week ending August 29 was negative almost everywhere. Closed sales averaged 456 a day, down 7.6% from the prior week. Pending sales averaged 458 a day, down 13.8%. New listings averaged 542 a day, down 15.2%. Of C.A.R.'s five regions only the Central Coast gained ground, at plus 9.8%. The Bay Area slipped 7.5%, the second smallest decline in the state.
• The one number worth your attention: 0.59. C.A.R. calls it the inventory replenishment rate, and it is the cleanest supply signal in the whole report. It is simply the 542 new listings divided by the 914 homes that either closed or went into contract. In plain English, for every 100 homes that left the market last week, only 59 arrived to replace them. Anything below 1.0 means inventory is shrinking.
• Rates are the reason. The 30-year fixed sat at 6.89% Friday and the 15-year at 6.49%. The 30-year's 52-week high is 6.91%, so we are two basis points off the top of the year. That is the entire statewide slowdown in one line.
NOW THE LOCAL PART, AND IT IS A DIFFERENT MARKET
Both charts above sit on one basis on purpose: C.A.R., July 2026, existing single family homes, statewide and city by city. A note on method, because it matters here. The weekly PDF prints a statewide 29 days on market, but that figure is weekly and covers all property types, so it cannot be set against a monthly single family city report. The matching number is 26, from C.A.R.'s own July report.
• Speed: the typical California home took 26 days to go under contract in July. Fremont took 12. Milpitas 12, San Jose 13, Hayward 14, Newark 16, Union City 16, Oakland 17. Every city on this side of the bay beat the state, most of them by roughly half.
• Price discipline: the typical California sale closed at 99.3% of asking, meaning the seller left 0.7% on the table. Not one local city did that. Fremont closed at 100.7%, Hayward at 101.4%, Milpitas at 100.9%, and Newark, Union City and San Jose landed exactly at asking. Oakland's 108.2% is real, but read it as a listing convention rather than heat: agents there price low on purpose to start a bidding war, and Oakland is also the slowest city in the set at 17 days.
• Fremont in July: 77 existing single family sales, up 6.9% from a year ago, at a median of $1.66M. That median is down 11.5% year over year, which sounds alarming until you remember it rests on 77 sales and swings with whatever happened to close that month. Active listings were 114, down 8.8%, and only 27.2% of them had cut their price.
OUR BOARD THIS MORNING
• Board wide: 2,618 listings active plus in contract across the fifteen cities we track, with 226 coming soon and 163 brand new.
• The five city ledger: 678 live listings, of which 521 are active, 105 coming soon and 51 new. Median list price is $1,068,000, and 267 of them are under $1M.
• One honest caveat before you quote the 12 days at a listing appointment. C.A.R.'s 12 measures homes that actually sold. Our ledger's median of 31 days measures the age of what is still sitting. Both are true and they answer different questions: quick homes leave quickly, so whatever remains gets older by definition. If you are pricing this week, the 12 sets the expectation and the 31 is the pile you have to stand out from.

WHERE YOUR MORTGAGE RATE ACTUALLY COMES FROM
Market Briefs spent Sunday on the plumbing underneath the 30-year fixed, and it is worth two minutes.
• The buyers changed. Foreign governments now hold about 40% of U.S. government debt, down from more than half around 2008. China's holdings are off roughly $400B since 2011 and Japan's share is down to 4%. Money market funds and hedge funds filled the gap, and hedge funds alone now hold about $2.4 trillion of Treasuries, up from $600B in 2014.
• Washington is buying its own paper. Treasury said on August 19 it would double its bond buybacks from $2B to at least $4B a session, running September 9 through November 4. Against a market worth roughly $30 trillion, that is a rounding error.
• Why a Fremont buyer should care: the 30-year Treasury crossed 5% in May and again in July, the highest since 2007, and mortgage rates track long yields far more closely than they track the Fed. The 10-year finished Friday at 4.78%. Until that number falls, 6.89% is roughly what the loan costs.
• The Fed meets September 16. After Friday's jobs report, futures put the odds of a rate hike near 58%, up from 49% the day before. That is the wrong direction for anyone waiting on cheaper money, and it is worth saying to clients plainly.
TWO HOUSING NUMBERS FROM THE NATIONAL FILE
• New home sales fell 10.5% in July to a 607,000 annual pace, a six month low, and the median new home price dropped to $393,800. We checked that against the Census series rather than taking it on faith: it is the lowest median in exactly five years, with July 2021 the last month at or below it. Builders are sitting on 9.6 months of supply and are cutting prices to move it. The West was the exception, up 6.2% on the month and 2.2% above last July.
• Rents are steadying. Apartment List's national median reached $1,390 in August, a seventh straight monthly rise and the first August increase since 2022, though still 0.8% below a year ago. Vacancy eased to 7.1%, the lowest since September 2025.
ONE CORRECTION TO THE SOURCE MATERIAL
C.A.R.'s weekly write up says consumer confidence fell 0.8 points "in July to 89.4 from a downwardly revised 90.2 in July." The reading is August's, not July's. The Conference Board published 89.4 for August, down from July's 90.2, with the Expectations Index off 5.8 points to 68.2. The numbers are right and the month label is wrong.
