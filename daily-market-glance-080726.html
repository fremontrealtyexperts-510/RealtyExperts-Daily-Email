Rates held. The 30-year fixed is still 6.77% and the 15-year is still 6.30%. Mortgage News Daily publishes its index late in the afternoon, so today's reading is not out yet and these are Thursday's numbers, unchanged from what we reported yesterday. Today's newsletter printed the same two figures, which is the first time this week it has agreed with the source on rates.
The big one for our business: United Wholesale Mortgage, the largest mortgage lender in the country, lost $452 million last quarter and ended its dividend. The stock closed down 34.78% after being off as much as 49% at one point during the day. Read the mechanism rather than the headline. Lenders earn on volume, and volume is a function of how many people are willing to borrow at 6.77%. Fewer loans means less revenue, and the biggest lender in the country just showed everyone what that looks like on a balance sheet. If the largest player is losing money at these rates, expect smaller lenders to tighten, merge, or quietly disappear over the next few quarters. That changes who your buyer can actually get a loan from.
HOA foreclosures are up nearly 40% from two years ago, and this one lands directly in transactions. Associations are being squeezed by rising insurance premiums, deferred repairs, and thin reserves, so they are moving faster against owners who fall behind on dues. Three practical consequences follow: higher monthly dues, more special assessments, and in the worst case deferred maintenance that drags comparable values down for everyone in the association. If you are writing an offer on anything with an HOA right now, the reserve study and the insurance line are not boilerplate. Read them.
Why a REALTOR® cares: both of today's stories are about the same thing, which is that the people who finance and maintain housing are under real pressure even though prices have held. Your buyer sees a listing. You should be seeing the lender behind the loan and the association behind the dues, because that is where this market is actually straining.

A quiet red day with one loud exception. The S&P 500 eased 0.18% to 7,710 and the NASDAQ barely moved, off 0.06% to 26,348. The Dow did the heavy lifting on the downside, falling 0.85% to 53,885.
One correction before we go further. Today's newsletter printed the Dow at 54,885. The actual close was 53,885, exactly 1,000 points lower. The percentage it published was right, so this looks like a typing slip rather than a bad feed, but anyone checking a statement against that number would think they had lost a thousand points overnight.
SpaceX was the real story. Early investors were finally allowed to sell yesterday when the lockup expired, releasing 911 million shares, roughly 7% of the company. Lockups exist so that everyone who got in early cannot cash out the moment a stock starts trading, and the usual result when one expires is a sell-off. Instead SpaceX rose 6.14%, which means buyers absorbed the entire wave. That is a genuine show of demand.
Earnings closed out the week unevenly. AppLovin dropped 19.66% after missing, blaming timing: its AI upgrades landed after the quarter ended. Peloton fell 15.57% despite reporting its first profitable quarter ever, because investors doubt it can repeat it. Warner Bros. Discovery rose 1.66% on streaming growth, and Hertz added 0.46% while still losing money, just less of it.
Apollo also agreed to buy British airline EasyJet for $7.7 billion. What it actually wants is the takeoff slots at crowded European airports, which are close to impossible to get any other way. It is the same logic as paying up for the corner lot. The dirt is not what makes it valuable, the location is.

The 10-Year is the August 6 Treasury close from the daily yield curve. Gold and silver are 5:15 AM Eastern quotes on August 7 against the same hour a day earlier. Brent is the August 6 ICE front-month settlement and copper is the August 6 COMEX settlement. Stocks are as of the August 6 close and crypto is a live quote from this morning. Metals move fast intraday, so treat these as a same day snapshot.
Copper just set a record, and the reason behind it matters more than the number. The August 5 close of $6.70 a pound is the highest in the COMEX series going back to 2000. Thursday eased back a fraction to $6.69, which is where the chart below ends, and copper is up 18.6% so far this year.
Normally copper at a record is the most reliable sign there is that the world economy is booming. It goes into everything that gets built: wire, pipe, motors, roofs. This time it is not that. The move is being driven by supply disruptions at the mines and by data centers buying enormous quantities for AI buildouts. Broad construction demand is not what is doing it, which is why nobody is celebrating. Some analysts think even the data center demand may prove temporary, since the industry is already shopping for alternatives to copper.
A note on that chart. The graphic going around put the price at $6.76 and titled it the highest price of 2026. Neither holds up. That $6.76 is an intraday tick from today, a session that had not settled when the picture was published, presented as Thursday's close. And Thursday was actually a down day, off 0.24%. We rebuilt every point from the COMEX settlement series rather than copy the picture. Today's newsletter was further off still, printing copper at $6.17 and saying it neared $6.90 a pound, when the highest tick in the whole series going back to 2000 is $6.77.
Silver went along for the ride and then some, jumping 4.07% to $64.35 an ounce. That is the largest one day move in the metals this week, and it is the same story as copper. Silver is half precious metal and half industrial input, and industrial metals are being bid hard right now. Gold added 0.81% to $4,305. The newsletter left both metals out of its report entirely for the third day running.
Brent reversed hard, up 3.83% to $82.49. Yesterday it fell on news that the Strait of Hormuz would partially reopen. Today Iran floated the actual terms: it wants U.S. and Israeli ships kept out, with fines up to 20% of cargo value for anyone who breaks the rule. About a third of the world's oil moves through that strait, so the market took the discount straight back.
The 10-Year rose to 4.69%, up 6 basis points. Today's newsletter printed 4.67%; Treasury's own daily curve says 4.69%, and we go with Treasury.
Jamie Dimon spent the day warning about leverage nobody can see. Margin debt, which is money investors borrow from their brokers to buy more stock, is at a record, and he thinks the true total is higher still once you count hedge funds and Treasury trades. The mechanism is simple enough. When prices fall, lenders demand their cash back, and borrowers have to sell to raise it, which pushes prices down further. That is how an ordinary decline turns into a fast one.
Why a REALTOR® cares: copper and oil both feed construction and materials costs, so a record in one and a 3.83% jump in the other is a quiet tax on every renovation bid and new build estimate your clients see this fall. The 10-Year moving up 6 basis points is the nearer term signal, because that is the number mortgage rates follow.

Barely moving. Bitcoin is sitting near $64,535, Ether is around $1,907, and XRP is the weak name again, off 2.67% to just over $1.01.
MARA, the largest publicly traded Bitcoin miner, lost money last quarter for a reason worth understanding. It holds roughly 35,000 Bitcoin worth about $2.1 billion, and accounting rules force it to write that pile down when prices fall. The mining operation itself did not break; the value of what it was already holding did. The stock fell 5.25% anyway.
The other story is a security failure that is not supposed to be possible. Hackers took $130 million out of a cold wallet, which is storage kept deliberately offline precisely so it cannot be reached remotely. The keys had been left online by mistake. The maker, Coinkite, now will not confirm how much was actually taken. A company that will not say how much it lost is usually telling you something about the size of the number.
