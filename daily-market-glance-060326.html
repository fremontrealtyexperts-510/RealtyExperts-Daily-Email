Mortgage Rates Hold At 6.57% As Job Openings Climb And Hiring Cools; East Bay Inventory Eases To 2,849 Active On A Lighter 210-New Wednesday
The 30-year fixed held at 6.57%, with the 15-year at 6.10% — a second straight day with no move. The data underneath is a tug-of-war: April job openings jumped to a one-year high even as hiring slipped and CEOs turned more cautious (see Economy), while the 10-year eased to 4.45%. None of it is decisive enough to pull rates meaningfully either way, so the buyer waiting for a dramatic drop is still waiting.
Locally, the East Bay board eased to 2,849 active, down 60 from Tuesday's 2,909, on a lighter 210 new listings and 216 closed sales. New supply pulled back broadly midweek while buyers kept right on closing at almost exactly yesterday's pace.
Two national notes worth passing along. Bill Pulte — who runs the Federal Housing Finance Agency and chairs Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the pair backing more than $10 trillion in U.S. mortgages — was named acting Director of National Intelligence while keeping his housing job. He is the same official who has pushed to lower mortgage rates and floated the idea of a 50-year mortgage. Separately, investors are piling into healthcare real estate: the deals are big, the cash flow is steady, and aging-population demand isn't going anywhere.

The labor market is sending mixed signals. Job openings jumped to 7.6 million in April, the highest since May 2024 — but hiring in the same month fell by 419,000 to 5.12 million. Those two usually move together, so the gap suggests employers are posting roles they are not actually filling.
The mood in the C-suite is cooling to match. The Conference Board's quarterly survey of 141 CEOs flipped from optimistic to pessimistic in a single quarter: only 15% now say the economy is in better shape than six months ago, down from 39% last quarter. And it is showing up in plans — 31% intend to cut headcount over the next year, against just 28% looking to add. The 10-year Treasury eased to 4.45% and gold firmed to $4,518.

Crypto had a rough session, with Bitcoin sliding 5.3% to $67,550 and the majors down right alongside it. The noise wasn't only price: JPMorgan (JPM +1.45%) CEO Jamie Dimon, a longtime advocate for tighter crypto rules, took aim at Coinbase's CEO over the industry-backed CLARITY Act. Dimon's line is simple — if a crypto firm takes deposits like a bank, it should be regulated like one.
Meanwhile, crypto is quietly encroaching on Wall Street's turf. A tool called perpetuals (‘perps’) — bets that never expire — is pulling volume from traditional exchanges and spreading into oil, stocks, even private names like SpaceX, per a TD Securities report. In one telling example, with oil markets closed over a weekend, crypto venue Hyperliquid called roughly 80% of the next move before the CME (CME -2.80%) even reopened.
