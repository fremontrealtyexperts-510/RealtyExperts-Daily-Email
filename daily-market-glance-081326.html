Rates finally broke the streak. The 30-year fixed slipped 5 basis points to 6.74% and the 15-year eased 1 to 6.27%, a five week low after five straight weeks of climbing. Today's newsletter printed the same two figures, matching Mortgage News Daily. Worth noting for anyone quoting a client this afternoon: the 30-year has kept falling today, reading 6.69% in the latest update, so the direction is still down as of this writing.
The cause is the inflation print. July prices rose just 0.1%, and cooler inflation is what lets the Fed consider cutting, which is what pulls mortgage rates down. The caveat is the same one as always: this relief window closes if the Iran conflict drags on and pushes energy costs back up.
Lumber tells a quieter story about where building is going. Prices have fallen from about $659 per thousand board feet in mid July to roughly $575 now, a drop of about 13%, even though they are still above where they started the year. The reason is not that demand collapsed. Mills cut output, and builders shifted toward multifamily projects, which use less wood per unit than single family homes. So the lumber market is telling you what builders decided to build, not just how much.
Why a REALTOR® cares: a five week low is a reason to call the buyers who paused. Not because 6.74% is a good rate in any absolute sense, but because a client who stopped looking at 6.83% has been waiting for a direction change, and this is the first one in over a month. The people who bought during the last dip are the ones who had already run their numbers and were ready to move on a Tuesday.

A quiet green day. The S&P 500 added 0.26% to 7,748, the NASDAQ gained 0.54% to 26,588, and the Dow was essentially flat, easing 0.04% to 53,770. The July inflation print landed and it was mild enough to let everyone relax.
Cisco is the lesson of the week, and it is a useful one. The company beat expectations, its shares rose 2.86% on August 12 to $123.88, and then the stock fell 8.4% the next session to $113.47. Nothing went wrong. The stock had already climbed roughly 61% this year, so a good quarter was not news, it was the minimum required to justify the price that had already been paid. See the chart below.
That gap between good results and a falling stock is worth understanding, because it is the same logic that governs a house that has been sitting. Price already contains an expectation. Meeting the expectation does nothing. You only move the number by beating it.
Wendy's had the strangest run of the day, up 14.70% on reports that Nelson Peltz's firm is working to take the burger chain private. This is the same stock a Reddit campaign pushed up 25% in June, and which then lost its spot as the second largest burger chain. Fast moves for fast food.
Overseas: the STOXX 600 rose 0.21%, the FTSE 100 slipped 0.27%, and China's CSI 300 fell 0.57%.
Cisco ran from $77.03 to $123.88 this year, up about 61%, and then gave back 8.4% in a single session to $113.47 after its earnings report. The company beat expectations. The stock fell anyway, which is what happens when a beat is already priced in.

Brent is the August 12 ICE front-month settlement. Stocks are as of the August 12 close, except Cisco where the August 13 close is shown, and crypto is a live quote from this morning.
Inflation cooled and nobody had to panic. Consumer prices rose just 0.1% in July, matching forecasts, after falling 0.4% in June. The annual rate is 3.4%, still above the Fed's 2% target but heading the right way. Shelter rose 0.1% and accounted for roughly two thirds of the entire monthly increase, which is a reminder that housing costs are most of what inflation actually is now.
The 10-Year eased to 4.68%, down 2 basis points. Today's newsletter printed 4.67% and down 1; Treasury's daily curve says 4.68% and down 2, and we go with Treasury.
The yen is the story worth watching, and the chart below shows why. The dollar buys about 159.4 yen, which is just under the 160 line where Japanese officials have intervened before. Japan has been buying yen to hold it up and the U.S. joined in a few weeks ago. The pair touched 163.86 in late July before pulling back. A weaker yen makes Japanese imports cheaper here and makes American goods more expensive there.
One number that should get more attention than it does: July's federal deficit hit $432.3 billion in a single month, driven by Medicare costs and interest on the national debt. Interest alone now runs about $1 trillion a year, roughly 14% of the entire federal budget. That is money that buys nothing.
Gold slipped 0.57% to $4,395 and silver fell 2.48% to $64.75, giving back part of a strong week. The newsletter left silver out again, so we sourced it ourselves as usual. Brent settled at $88.98, essentially flat. Today's newsletter printed $87.37 and down 1.83%, which is neither the settlement nor the prior close, so we go with the settled ICE front-month figure.
Why a REALTOR® cares: a 0.1% inflation print is the kind of number that lets the Fed cut later this year, and shelter being two thirds of it cuts both ways. It means the single biggest lever on inflation is the cost of housing, so the path to lower rates runs directly through the market you sell in.
The yen has weakened about 4.3% against the dollar over six months, touching 163.86 in late July before settling back to 159.38, just under the 160 line where Tokyo has stepped in before. A higher line means a weaker yen.

Flat across the board. Bitcoin sits near $63,355, Ether is around $1,886, and XRP is just above $1.01. Three assets, three moves smaller than a fifth of a percent.
The real news is who is showing up. JPMorgan and nearly 40 other firms are testing tokenized assets, which are digital versions of real things like a stock or a bond. The market is already around $38 billion, and the Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation, which is the plumbing underneath most American securities settlement, plans to run it as a regular service starting in October. When the settlement layer of Wall Street adopts the technology, that is a different kind of milestone than a price move.
Balanced against that, a reminder of how young the plumbing still is. Somebody drained 200,000 XRP, about $200,000, from a bridge that moves tokens between blockchains, because the bridge never checked whether the deposit actually arrived. It took 97 minutes. The flaw was in a tool built on the XRP Ledger rather than in XRP itself, which is the distinction that matters and the one that never makes the headline.
