Two national items are worth your attention.
• Wall Street's new landlord: Goldman Sachs is buying LCN Capital for $410 million and picking up more than 375 rental properties around the country. LCN owns them but does not manage them, so Goldman is entering the kind of landlord business that never hears from a tenant.
• Frozen housing, solid quarter: Home Depot beat both profit and sales forecasts, with same store sales up 1.7% for its best showing in four years, and still described the housing market as rough. Customers have the money and are hesitating on the big projects.
That second point is the one that travels. It is the same standoff we see locally: real demand, slow trigger fingers. Buyers and owners are not short on capacity, they are short on urgency, and that is a very different problem from the one we had two years ago. When the hesitation breaks, it tends to break quickly.

A broad pullback with technology taking the worst of it. The S&P 500 fell 0.69% to 7,691, the Dow eased 0.22% to 53,343, and the NASDAQ dropped 1.33% to 26,289.
Energy went the other way. The sector is back near record highs because oil keeps getting pricier, and it has climbed 20% from its July low. Valero and Marathon are each up more than 100% so far this year.
Staffing firms also bounced back after months of investors worrying that artificial intelligence would replace them. Manpower rose 4.77% and Robert Half added 3.04% on better than expected earnings, with Manpower posting its best operating profit in three years. The reason is refreshingly simple: companies still need people to recruit, hire and manage temporary staff.

The Port of Los Angeles moved 960,464 TEUs in July, the second-busiest July in the port's history and behind only July 2025. Retailers pulled shipments forward to get ahead of tariffs and to dodge Iran tensions and Panama Canal delays. The footnote the headline leaves out is that volume was still down 5.82% from last July. Fuller shelves now, with shipping costs that could still reach prices later.
Separately, the IRS clarified the no tax on overtime deduction. More than 29 million people claimed it in 2025 at roughly $3,000 apiece, but the break is not unlimited. Anything above $12,500 can still be taxed, and employers must keep tracking overtime on W-2s.
