Mortgage Rates Ease To 6.60% As The Dow Notches A Record; Manhattan Luxury Shrugs Off A New Second-Home Tax; The East Bay Board Slips To 2,748 Active On 95 New And 216 Closed
The 30-year fixed eased to 6.60% and the 15-year to 6.17%. A small step down, but rates in this range keep the lock-in effect firmly in place, so owners stay put rather than trade a low rate for a higher one, and resale supply stays tight.
At the high end, Manhattan's luxury market is shrugging off a new tax on second homes. Luxury inventory has dropped about 40% from last year to its lowest since 2004, and wealthy cash buyers are scooping up what is left, tax or no tax. Closer to home, California voters will weigh a one-time 5% tax on billionaires later this year.
Looking further out, the Mortgage Bankers Association says the housing shortage could flip to a surplus by 2035, as slower population growth and fewer new buyers cool demand. That would mean more choices and softer prices down the road, though that is a story for the next decade, not this one.
Locally, the East Bay board slipped to 2,748 active (down 46 from Friday), with just 95 new listings over the holiday weekend against 216 closings. Oakland again led the board with 21 new on 725 active; Fremont added 10 new on 330 active.

For agents: a holiday week always runs quiet, so do not read too much into the thin new-listing count. Rates ticked down, stocks are at record highs, and buyers are feeling a little wealthier. Well-priced, well-presented homes still move inside about four weeks, so the coaching holds: price to today's market and the sale follows.

Stocks are back in action after the long holiday weekend, and the Dow is starting it at a record. It closed last week at an all-time high and sits at 52,900, up 10.06% on the year (see today's chart). The S&P 500 held flat at 7,483 while the NASDAQ eased 0.80% to 25,832.
Not everyone is sticking around, though. Investors pulled $17.2 billion out of U.S. stock funds last week, the fastest pace in months. With markets at highs, some are cashing out and some are bracing for turbulence.
The AI trade hit a snag too. Mark Zuckerberg admitted Meta's (META -4.90%) AI progress has been slower than expected and said morale is at a low point, even as the company plans to spend $135 billion on AI this year. That is a big bet, and some investors are nervous.
And a reminder that no job is fully safe: in China, AI-powered quant funds beat human stock pickers by more than 20 percentage points last year. The machines are coming for Wall Street too.
The Dow closed at a record 52,900, up 10.06% year-to-date (+4,836.78), after an April low near 45,300 and a steady climb back.

Stocks and the 10-Year as of the July 2nd close; gold, silver, and Brent are spot as of July 5th to 6th (Market Briefs, Trading Economics).
Metals kept climbing. Gold rose about 1.49% to $4,187 and silver jumped 2.77% to $62.75 after a soft June jobs report cooled the odds of a Fed hike. The 10-year Treasury yield held at 4.48% and Brent crude was flat at $72.12.
The tax story of the week is Ireland. The country pulled in about $16.8 billion in corporate taxes in the first half of this year, and three U.S. companies paid nearly half of it. The draw is obvious: Ireland's 12.5% corporate rate is roughly half the OECD average of 24.2% and well below the U.S. 25.6% (see today's chart).
Closer to daily life, a heat wave is straining the power grid across the central and eastern U.S. New England wholesale electricity prices spiked 243% in a single day as millions cranked up the AC. One bit of relief: the UN food-price index slipped 0.3% in June, led by grains and sugar, as post-ceasefire supply starts flowing again.
Ireland's 12.5% corporate rate sits far below the OECD average of 24.2% and the U.S. 25.6%, which is why so much corporate profit books there.
