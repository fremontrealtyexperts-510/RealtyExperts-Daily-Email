Rates barely moved. The 30-year fixed eased 1 basis point to 6.79% and the 15-year eased 1 to 6.28%, per Mortgage News Daily. Today's newsletter printed the same two figures.
The number that matters today is delinquency. The share of mortgage balances newly falling behind reached 3.95% in the second quarter, the highest since the second quarter of 2015. That is a genuine decade high, and we checked it in the New York Fed's own data rather than taking the headline. Households are still paying, but the group that cannot is the largest it has been in ten years.
The chart below shows where that stress actually sits, and it is not spread evenly. FHA loans ran an 11.88% delinquency rate in the most recent Mortgage Bankers Association survey, against 2.75% for conventional loans. That is a 913 basis point gap, the widest since 2021, and it is more than four times the conventional rate. VA loans sit at 4.99%. All loans together came in at 4.44%.
That split is the practical part. FHA is the low down payment, lower credit score path, so it carries the buyers with the thinnest cushion. When those borrowers stumble first and hardest, it is not a housing market failure so much as a reminder of who was stretched to get in. Conventional delinquencies actually improved 14 basis points on the quarter.
Meanwhile the market itself keeps grinding. July existing home sales fell 1.7% and the median price still set a July record at $434,100, up 2.0% from a year ago and the 37th straight month of annual gains. Fewer sales at higher prices is the signature of tight inventory, not healthy demand.
Why a REALTOR® cares: if you have FHA buyers, this is the quarter to be honest about reserves. A buyer who qualifies on paper with nothing left over after closing is exactly the profile in that 11.88%. Ask what happens to the payment if the car breaks or the hours get cut, and if there is no answer, that is a conversation worth having before the offer instead of after the first missed payment.

A soft, red day across the board. The S&P 500 slipped 0.32% to 7,728, the Dow gave back 0.34% to 53,791, and the NASDAQ dropped 0.60% to 26,445. Nothing broke, but nothing held either.
Everybody was waiting rather than trading. The July inflation reading lands this morning, and it is the number that decides whether the Fed has room to cut. When a data point that big is a few hours away, most desks simply stop taking risk. That is what a 0.3% down day with no story behind it usually means.
Earnings were the only real news. Super Micro rose 0.45% after orders came in ahead of expectations, CoreWeave added 2.42% on a cloud and AI quarter strong enough to name Anthropic and Meta as new customers, and Cava fell 1.25% even though sales grew, because customers adding extras is a thinner story than customers showing up.
The detail worth keeping is about leveraged AI ETFs. These funds hold roughly 1% of global ETF assets but generate about 16% of daily ETF trading volume. A sliver of the money is doing a sixth of the trading, and nearly all of it is concentrated in a handful of AI names. That is why the index can lurch on days when nothing actually happened. It is a small tail attached to a very large dog.
Overseas was quiet in the same way: the STOXX 600 finished flat, the FTSE 100 eased 0.17%, and China's CSI 300 fell 0.81%.

Brent is the August 11 ICE front-month settlement. Stocks are as of the August 11 close and crypto is a live quote from this morning. The household debt figures are the New York Fed's Q2 2026 report, released August 11.
The New York Fed published its quarterly read on household debt yesterday, and it is the most useful thing on the wire today. Total household debt actually fell slightly to $18.8 trillion. The trouble is underneath that number.
Non-housing debt, the part of the balance sheet that has nothing to do with a mortgage, hit $5.20 trillion. Credit card balances rose $21 billion to $1.26 trillion, and auto loans rose $28 billion to $1.71 trillion. Student loans went the other way, down $7 billion to $1.65 trillion. See the chart below for how those four pieces have grown since 2003.
One note on that chart. The version going around with this story was labeled the first quarter, and the first quarter is a stale frame now that the second quarter release is out. It also printed auto loans at $1.65 trillion and student loans at $1.63 trillion, when the actual first quarter figures were $1.69 trillion and $1.66 trillion. We rebuilt every point from the Fed's own workbook so the numbers and the quarter both match the release.
The delinquency picture is genuinely split. Overall, 4.7% of all household debt is in some stage of delinquency, and that is slightly better than last quarter. But 12.9% of credit card balances are now at least 90 days past due. So the average borrower is fine and improving, while a specific group of borrowers is falling apart. An aggregate that improves while its worst slice deteriorates is not a calm reading, it is two different economies inside one number.
On the rest of the board: the 10-Year eased to 4.70%, down 2 basis points. Today's newsletter printed 4.69% and called it unchanged; Treasury's daily curve says 4.70% and down 2, and we go with Treasury. Brent settled at $88.91, up 1.36%, still elevated because the Hormuz route is still contested and Iran says nothing moves until the blockade lifts. The newsletter printed $89.22 and up 1.71%, which is a live tick rather than the settlement.
Gold ran to $4,420 an ounce, up 1.01%, and silver jumped 2.21% to $66.40, its highest in about seven weeks. The newsletter left silver out again, which is now routine enough that we simply source it ourselves.
Why a REALTOR® cares: card balances and auto loans are the two debts that move a buyer's debt to income ratio fastest, and they are both climbing. A client who carried a $6,000 card balance at the pre-approval and $11,000 by the time they write an offer can lose the approval without their income changing at all. This is the quarter to tell buyers to stop opening credit, not to spend the summer furnishing the house they have not bought yet.

Flat for Bitcoin, better for the rest. Bitcoin sits near $63,415, Ether gained 1.53% to about $1,891, and XRP added 0.78% to just over $1.01.
The real story is a Texas Bitcoin mine turning into a landlord. Riot struck a $9.1 billion deal with Anthropic to supply power and data center capacity, and the stock rose 4.33% on it. The logic is almost too tidy: AI labs desperately need grid power and cooled buildings, and crypto miners spent a decade acquiring exactly those two things for a business that is currently in a downturn. So the miners are renting out the infrastructure instead of using it.
That is a real pivot, not a press release. It also quietly changes what a mining company is. If the power contract pays better than the mining, the Bitcoin part becomes the side business.
Elsewhere, the Clarity Act has been delayed, so the rulebook that would have told the SEC how to treat crypto is not arriving on schedule and the agency keeps improvising. Meanwhile there is talk of stocks moving toward the around the clock trading that crypto already has, which sounds modern until you imagine your portfolio moving at 3 AM while you sleep.
