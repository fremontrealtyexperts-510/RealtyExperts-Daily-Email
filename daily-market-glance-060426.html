Mortgage Rates Tick Up To 6.61% As Tariff Threats Widen And Savings Thin; East Bay Inventory Climbs To 2,901 Active On A Steady 214-New Thursday
The 30-year fixed nudged up to 6.61% and the 15-year to 6.13%, a modest move higher after holding flat earlier in the week. The backdrop explains the drift: fresh tariff threats on 60 trading partners and a PCE inflation reading running at a three-year high (see Economy) keep upward pressure on rates, even as the 10-year Treasury sat roughly steady at 4.49%. None of it is decisive, but the direction this week was up, not down — another reminder that the buyer waiting for a clean break lower is still waiting.
Locally, the East Bay board climbed to 2,901 active, up 52 from Wednesday's 2,849, on 214 new listings and 204 closed sales. Inventory is building again: new supply held about steady (214 vs 210) while closings cooled (204 vs 216), so more homes are coming on than are clearing.
A couple of national notes worth passing along. In San Francisco, a home listed at $7.95 million just sold for $15 million — and prices there are up nearly 15% year-over-year, the fastest of any major U.S. metro, with the AI-IPO wave not even fully underway yet. Separately, big money keeps flowing into industrial real estate: investment firms Kayne Anderson and BKM just bought a $1.8 billion light-industrial portfolio, a sign the warehouse trade is back in favor.

It was a red session for the major indexes, with all three closing lower on Wednesday. Two single-stock stories stood out. Broadcom (AVGO -0.49%) posted record quarterly sales of $22.2 billion — up 48% from a year ago — but the stock still slid as much as 8% after hours. The catch: shares had already run up 15% and added roughly $280 billion in market value over the prior four sessions, and when the company held its $100 billion AI-chip target steady instead of raising it, traders took profits.
GameStop (GME +6.02%) went the other way after authorizing a $2 billion share buyback. A buyback is simply a company purchasing its own stock — here funded from the $8.4 billion in cash and securities GameStop has held since the 2021 meme rally. Fewer shares outstanding means each remaining share is a slightly bigger slice of the company, and the market rewarded the move.

Trade tension is back in the headlines. The White House is proposing tariffs of at least 10% on imports from 60 trading partners — including the E.U., China, Canada, and Mexico — this time framed around a 1974 trade law by arguing those partners rely on forced labor. Two earlier tariff attempts were struck down in court, so the legal footing matters as much as the economics here.
The household picture is softening underneath. The U.S. personal savings rate fell to 2.6% in April, the lowest since June 2022, as families leaned on stashed cash to cover rent, food, and gas. At the same time the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the PCE, rose 3.8% — its fastest pace in three years. Thinner savings alongside firmer inflation is an uncomfortable mix. Abroad, the OECD warned that a prolonged Iran conflict and disruption at the Strait of Hormuz could drag global growth to 1.8%, which outside of COVID and 2008 would be the weakest in 40 years. The 10-year Treasury held at 4.49% and gold firmed to $4,483.

Crypto firmed slightly but stayed under pressure. Bitcoin ticked up 0.3% to $64,253 yet remains pinned near the floor of its 'Power Law corridor' — a model that maps where Bitcoin has historically tended to trade. Market Briefs notes the only two prior touches of that level were the COVID crash in March 2020 and the FTX collapse in late 2022; whether the pattern holds carries no guarantee.
Ether ($1,814, +0.1%) and XRP ($1.22, +0.4%) posted similarly modest gains. For most buyers and sellers of homes the read-through is indirect, but crypto swings still color the risk appetite of a slice of Bay Area buyers, so it's worth keeping on the radar.
